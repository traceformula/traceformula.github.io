Rules

Rules are written B/S: how many neighbors cause a birth, and how many let a cell survive. Conway's is B3/S23.

Invent your own

What is this?

In 1970, mathematician John Conway invented a "game" with no players. You set up a grid of cells, and from then on four simple rules decide everything.

The four rules

LonelyA living cell with fewer than 2 living neighbors dies.
StableA living cell with 2 or 3 living neighbors survives.
CrowdedA living cell with more than 3 living neighbors dies.
BirthA dead cell with exactly 3 living neighbors comes alive.

Why people care

  • It is Turing complete. A big enough pattern can compute anything an ordinary computer can.
  • Because of that, no general method can predict whether a given pattern will eventually die out. Often the only way to know is to run it.
  • Conway offered a $50 prize for a pattern that grows forever. Bill Gosper's team at MIT won in 1970 with the glider gun, which you can load here.
  • It reached the public through Martin Gardner's column in Scientific American in October 1970.
  • Some patterns, called Gardens of Eden, cannot arise from any earlier state. They can only exist as a starting point.
  • People have built Life patterns that simulate Life itself, so a whole grid acts as one giant cell.
  • Conway died of COVID-19 in April 2020. He had mixed feelings about being known mainly for Life, given his deeper work in mathematics.

Reading the colors

Bright cells were just born. They cool as they age, so stable structures look calm and active regions look hot. Faint trails show cells that recently died.
